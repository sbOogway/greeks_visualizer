A visual guide to option Greeks

The Greeks are just slopes.

An option's price depends on a handful of things: the stock price, how jumpy the stock is, how much time is left, and interest rates. Each Greek answers one simple question: if one of those things moves a little and everything else stays put, how much does the option's price change? In calculus that is called a partial derivative. This page lets you see each one.

Before we start

Three ideas, no prior calculus needed

A call option is the right to buy a stock at a fixed price K (the strike) on a future date. A put is the right to sell at K. Its price today depends on the inputs in the panel.

A derivative is a rate of change

Your car's speedometer shows a derivative: how fast your position is changing right now. Delta is the option's speedometer for stock moves: dollars of option per dollar of stock.

"Partial" means one knob at a time

Think of a mixing desk. Turn one knob, leave the others alone, and listen to what changes. Each Greek turns exactly one knob: stock price, volatility, time, or rates.

A second derivative is the change of the change

Acceleration is how fast your speed changes. Gamma is how fast delta changes when the stock moves.

A car that pulls away, then cruises

Press play, or drag sideways on the charts to scrub through time. The faint dots on the road mark where the car was at each second: the gaps grow while it speeds up, then stay equal once it cruises.

The key twist: for a car the thing that changes is time. For delta and gamma it is the stock price, so think of the stock price as the option's clock. The option also has a real "velocity through time": that's theta, Θ = ∂V/∂t. And because the option's price depends on several inputs at once, we write the curly ∂ (partial) to say which one is moving.

§1 · The five Greeks

Five questions about your option

Each card asks one "what if" question and answers it for the option in the panel. The small formula underneath is the same question written in calculus.

In car terms

A car's dashboard has one main gauge, the speedometer, because its position changes with one thing: time. An option's price depends on five things, so it gets five gauges. Delta is a speedometer for stock moves, vega for volatility moves, theta for the clock ticking, and rho for rate moves. Gamma is the accelerometer sitting next to the delta gauge.

Go deeper: the Black–Scholes formula behind these numbers

All of the numbers come from one formula. You don't need to memorise it; the point is that it is an ordinary function, so we can take its derivatives.

\[ C = S e^{-q\tau} N(d_1) - K e^{-r\tau} N(d_2), \qquad d_1 = \frac{\ln(S/K) + (r - q + \tfrac12\sigma^2)\,\tau}{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}}, \quad d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{\tau} \]

S stock price, K strike, σ volatility, τ years to expiry, r interest rate, q dividend yield. N is the bell-curve probability function. A partial derivative nudges one input by a tiny amount h and keeps the rest fixed:

\[ \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{V(S + h,\ \sigma,\ \tau,\ r) - V(S,\ \sigma,\ \tau,\ r)}{h} \]
§2 · Delta and gamma

Delta is how steep the price curve is

The top chart shows what the option is worth at every stock price. Delta is how steep that curve is where you're standing, shown by the blue line that just touches it. A steep curve means the option moves almost dollar-for-dollar with the stock. A flat curve means it barely reacts.

Now plot that steepness for every stock price: that's the middle chart, delta. How steep is that curve? That's gamma, at the bottom. Gamma is biggest near the strike, where the price curve bends the most. Drag sideways on any chart to move the stock price.

In car terms

In physics class you plot a car's position against time, and velocity is the slope of that graph. Plot velocity, and its slope is acceleration. The three charts below are the same idea, with the stock price playing the role of time. They even have the same shapes as the car above.

Far to the left a call is parked (Δ ≈ 0: the stock moves, the option doesn't). Far to the right it is cruising at full speed, keeping pace with the stock dollar for dollar (Δ ≈ 1). Near the strike it is accelerating hardest, which is where gamma peaks. A put is the same car in reverse: its delta runs from −1 up to 0.

Measure the slope yourself

A computer measures slope the way you would with a ruler. Check the price a step h to the right and a step to the left, then divide the rise by the run. The smaller the step, the closer you get to the exact Δ.

In car terms: it's like working out speed from two mile markers, the distance between them divided by the time it took. Time a shorter stretch and you get closer to what the speedometer says.

§3 · What "partial" means

Walking on a price landscape

Let two inputs change instead of one, and the option's price becomes a landscape. Stock price runs one way, volatility the other, and the height is the option's value.

Stand on the black dot. Walk along the blue path, where only the stock price changes, and the steepness under your feet is delta. Walk along the orange path, where only volatility changes, and the steepness is vega. You only ever walk in one direction at a time, and that's what partial means.

In car terms

Picture driving across a hillside. How steep the road feels depends on which way you point the car: head east and you feel one slope, head north and you feel another. A road sign saying "8% grade" is a partial derivative, the steepness in one chosen direction. Delta is the grade when you drive in the stock-price direction, and vega the grade in the volatility direction. The tangent plane is the patch of hillside under your wheels, pressed flat.

§4 · Greeks of Greeks

Take the slope again, and again

Every Greek is itself a number that changes, so you can ask how it moves. Gamma is the delta of delta. Vanna asks how delta changes when volatility moves. Each arrow below means "how does this change when ___ moves?"

Here's a neat fact. Where two arrows meet, the order doesn't matter. "How does delta change with volatility?" and "how does vega change with the stock price?" give exactly the same number. Click Vanna to check.

In car terms

Take the derivative of acceleration and you get jerk, the lurch you feel when a driver stabs the brakes. The Greek called speed (confusingly) is the option's jerk: the third derivative of price with respect to the stock.

Loosely, "order doesn't matter" is like driving around a city block. Two blocks east then one north puts you on the same corner as one north then two east. Vanna gets to the same number whichever input you nudge first.

§5 · Putting the Greeks to work

Predict the new price without a pricing model

Say the stock jumps $5, volatility rises 2 points and a week goes by. Each Greek predicts its own share of the change: delta covers the stock move, vega the volatility move, theta the passing time. Add the shares up and you have a quick estimate.

For small moves that's nearly perfect. For big moves the curves bend, and the second-order terms (gamma and friends) correct for it. The bottom rows compare both estimates with the true new price.

In car terms

Where will the car be in 3 seconds? A first guess is where it is now plus velocity × time. If it's speeding up, add ½ × acceleration × time². That formula from school physics is a Taylor expansion, and the option version has exactly the same shape:

carxnew ≈ x + v·dt + ½a·dt² optionVnew ≈ V + Δ·dS + ½Γ·dS²

The other rows repeat the same trick for the inputs a car doesn't have: volatility, time and rates.

Go deeper: the formula (a Taylor expansion)
\[ dV \approx \underbrace{\Delta\,dS + \nu\,d\sigma + \Theta\,dt + \rho\,dr}_{\text{first order: slopes}} \;+\; \underbrace{\tfrac12\Gamma\,dS^2 + \text{vanna}\,dS\,d\sigma + \tfrac12\text{volga}\,d\sigma^2 + \text{charm}\,dS\,dt}_{\text{second order: bends and cross effects}} \]
§6 · How the Greeks fit together

Theta pays for gamma

Owning an option is like holding a ticket that pays off when the stock moves around. Gamma is how much you gain from those moves. Theta is the rent you pay every day to keep holding the ticket.

Black–Scholes sets the rent so that, for a typical day's move, the two cancel out exactly. The bars below show the balance for your option: the four pieces always add up to zero.

In car terms

Here the analogy stretches, so switch cars: think of a taxi. You pay the lease and fuel every day whether anyone hails you or not; that's theta. You earn on every ride, and a ride pays whichever direction it goes; that's gamma, which earns on stock moves up or down. Black–Scholes sets the lease so that on an ordinary day of rides, you break even.

Go deeper: the Black–Scholes equation

The balance above is a partial differential equation that every Black–Scholes price satisfies:

\[ \underbrace{\frac{\partial V}{\partial t}}_{\Theta} + \tfrac12\sigma^2 S^2 \underbrace{\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2}}_{\Gamma} + (r - q)\,S\,\underbrace{\frac{\partial V}{\partial S}}_{\Delta} - rV = 0 \]
Prices and Greeks use the Black–Scholes–Merton formula for European options with a continuous dividend yield. The "measured" checks nudge the inputs by small steps and recompute. Theta, charm, color and veta measure the effect of time passing, so they run opposite to "time left" τ.
